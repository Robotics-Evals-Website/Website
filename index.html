02 / three areas where measurement matters

What to build.

Three areas, three questions: how much paid physical work robots can do, how much of AI's own build-and-maintain loop they can close, and whether any of it is legible to people outside a lab. The examples anchor the idea; proposals in the same spirit are welcome.

A

Impact on jobs

Economically meaningful, contact-rich manipulation: the work robots would be paid to do. It's a direct signal of where automation is moving from demo to deployment, and how fast that frontier is expanding.

  • CableBench: seat multiple cables and barrel/RJ connectors into a port panel; randomize port position, orientation, panel tilt, connector type, cable slack.
  • TorqueBench: drive fasteners to a specified torque without cross-threading; randomize size, head type, hole location, approach angle.
  • FoldBench: fold a laid-out garment to a target footprint; randomize fabric, size, and initial crumple state.
  • ClutterPick: retrieve a named item from a bin under heavy occlusion; randomize clutter density, item pose, distractors.
  • HarnessRoute: route a flexible wire harness through a sequence of clips and channels; randomize clip positions, order, stiffness.
  • PlateBench: portion and arrange food-like items to a target layout; randomize count, size, start positions.
B

AI timelines

How much of the physical loop behind AI progress robots can close without people. Every step a machine takes unassisted, whether building robots, servicing compute, or running experiments, removes a human bottleneck from the feedback loop, so the trend line here is direct evidence about how fast takeoff could go.

  • SelfBuildBench: assemble a working copy of a reference arm from printed parts and stock hardware; randomize part presentation, fastener set, assembly order.
  • MechanicBench: restore another robot to working order by swapping a battery, replacing a worn part, or tightening loose fixtures; randomize the fault set and component positions.
  • RackBench: mount a compute unit into rails and connect power and data; randomize rail height, unit depth, cable dressing.
  • HotSwapBench: remove and re-seat a hot-swappable module in a live rack; randomize bay, latch mechanism, insertion force.
  • LabBench: execute a wet-lab protocol step (pipette, weigh, transfer) to a stated tolerance; randomize vessel layout, volumes, tool positions.
  • ReloadBench: replace a depleted consumable (spool, filter, cartridge) and confirm restored operation; randomize type, seating depth.
C

Societal awareness

Tasks anyone can watch and judge as done or not done, no expertise required. When capability is legible, it is far harder to over- or under-state how fast things are moving.

  • CoffeeBench: find a cup, load a pod, operate the machine, deliver the cup to a target zone (the Wozniak coffee test, made reproducible); randomize machine layout, cup/pod positions, target.
  • GroceryBench: unpack a bag of mixed groceries and shelve each item correctly; randomize item set, packing order, shelf layout.
  • TableBench: lay a place setting to a target arrangement; randomize starting positions, orientation, setting style.
  • DishBench: load mixed dishware into a rack without collisions or breakage; randomize dish types, counts, poses.
  • TidyBench: return scattered household objects to designated storage; randomize object set, clutter, target bins.
  • PourBench: pour a target volume into a specified vessel without overshoot or spill; randomize vessel, fill target, placement.

03 / why this exists

There's no standard way to measure a robot's real capabilities.

Every frontier lab scores its own systems on its own private tasks, then ships the demo that looks best. There's no shared, reproducible way to compare them, so no one outside the labs can say how capable robots really are, or how fast that's changing.

No shared benchmark. No public baseline. No way to check the demo.

This registry is the front end of the public record we're building. It starts empty by design; filling it, benchmark by benchmark, is what this program is for.

04 / the rule that defines this program

Benchmark = distribution

A task instance is a distribution, not a scene.

A benchmark task is a recipe: named variables with stated distributions. Realize it under a seed to get one fair sample. Re-seed for a new sample of the same task. That is how you measure generalization, not memorization.

Recipe

CableBench.v1
panel     460 × 300 mm  (fixed)
n_ports   ~ Categorical{3, 4, 5}
pos       ~ Uniform(panel)  gap ≥ 96mm
θ         ~ Uniform(−30°, +30°)
type      ~ Categorical{USB-C,
            Barrel, XLR, RJ45, BNC}
clutter   ~ Bernoulli(0.4)
lighting  ~ Uniform(2700, 6500 K)
──────────────────────────
sample = realize(CableBench.v1,
                   seed)

08 / questions

      
Frequently asked.

      

        
Who can apply?
Teams building robotics evaluations. You pick one of two tracks (Simulation or Real-world hardware) and one of three areas: impact on jobs, AI timelines, or societal awareness. Strong newcomers and established researchers are both welcome; what matters is a rigorous, reproducible benchmark.

        
What exactly does a Stage 1 submission need to contain?
An evaluation proposal that includes one fully specified task instance in our required format: a goal, setup variables each with a stated distribution, a target, and success criteria. The instance must be a distribution (a seeded recipe), not a single fixed scene. Hardware proposals must also run on an accepted reference robot (single-arm or bimanual), stay within the ~$1,000 bill-of-materials cap, and specify environment tolerances, a reset procedure, and a rollout count.

        
What exactly is due at the end of Stage 2?
Everything an independent team would need to rerun your benchmark and reproduce your numbers, due 11 December 2026: the finished benchmark running on Inspect Robots, its task specs and documentation, and the seeded rollout results behind every score you report. Hardware benchmarks must also document the reset procedure and environment tolerances they were run under. Our replication attempt runs against exactly this package and nothing else, so anything a replicator would need has to be in it.

        
How do payments and milestones work?
Stage 1 pays $500 for an accepted proposal that advances (genuine-but-not-selected proposals receive $250, capped at 50; spam or low-effort receives $0). Stage 2 pays by milestone. Simulation: up to $2,000 on delivery, plus $1,000 in compute credits on acceptance. Hardware: $2,000 on spec approval, $8,000 on a working benchmark, and $5,000–$22,000 on passing replication and quality tier, plus $1,000 in compute credits upfront and up to $1,000 in materials reimbursed against receipts. Hardware teams also receive two fully assembled SO-101 follower arms in kind, which do not count against the materials cap. All figures are per-team maximums.

        
How is the final hardware quality-tier amount decided?
By a rubric published on the first day of the program, so you know the scoring standard before you build. The replication-plus-quality-tier milestone pays between $5,000 and $22,000 based on that rubric, with the $5,000 base approved as soon as our rerun lands within your stated tolerance. The rubric scores task-family difficulty, documentation quality, stochasticity of instances, and rollout count.

        
Which robots can I use for a hardware benchmark?
An accepted reference robot. Arms: YAM, SO-101, WidowX AI (Trossen), Franka Research 3. Humanoids: AgiBot X1, Unitree G1. The accepted reference robots are confirmed on day one.

        
How are benchmarks licensed and released?
Everything is released openly. Code is licensed under MIT; data and documentation under CC-BY. QA and independent reproduction checks run after final deliverables are due on 11 December 2026; final payments are settled and the full public repository is released in mid-January 2027.

        
What does "an instance is a distribution" actually mean?
Instead of one rehearsed scene, you define named random variables with stated distributions, realized under a seed. Each seed produces a new but fair sample of the same task, and a score is an expectation over many seeded rollouts. This measures whether a system generalizes, rather than whether it memorized one setup.

        
What is Inspect Robots, and who runs the program?
Inspect Robots is the shared evaluation harness that benchmarks are built into: define a benchmark once, run any policy on any supported robot or sim, and get reproducible logs. The program is run by RoboCurve, independent of any frontier lab.
