02 / three areas where measurement matters

What to build.

We fund benchmarks in these three areas; the examples are illustrative.

A

Impact on jobs

Economically meaningful, contact-rich manipulation: the work robots would be paid to do. It marks where automation moves from demo to deployment, and how fast.

  • CableBench: seat multiple cables and barrel/RJ connectors into a port panel; randomize port position, orientation, panel tilt, connector type, cable slack.
  • TorqueBench: drive fasteners to a specified torque without cross-threading; randomize size, head type, hole location, approach angle.
  • FoldBench: fold a laid-out garment to a target footprint; randomize fabric, size, and initial crumple state.
  • ClutterPick: retrieve a named item from a bin under heavy occlusion; randomize clutter density, item pose, distractors.
  • HarnessRoute: route a flexible wire harness through a sequence of clips and channels; randomize clip positions, order, stiffness.
  • PlateBench: portion and arrange food-like items to a target layout; randomize count, size, start positions.
B

AI timelines

How much of the physical loop behind AI progress robots can close without people. Every step a machine takes unassisted, whether building robots, servicing compute, or running experiments, removes a human bottleneck from the feedback loop.

  • SelfBuildBench: assemble a working copy of a reference arm from printed parts and stock hardware; randomize part presentation, fastener set, assembly order.
  • MechanicBench: restore another robot to working order by swapping a battery, replacing a worn part, or tightening loose fixtures; randomize the fault set and component positions.
  • RackBench: mount a compute unit into rails and connect power and data; randomize rail height, unit depth, cable dressing.
  • HotSwapBench: remove and re-seat a hot-swappable module in a live rack; randomize bay, latch mechanism, insertion force.
  • LabBench: execute a wet-lab protocol step (pipette, weigh, transfer) to a stated tolerance; randomize vessel layout, volumes, tool positions.
  • ReloadBench: replace a depleted consumable (spool, filter, cartridge) and confirm restored operation; randomize type, seating depth.
C

Societal awareness

Tasks anyone can watch and judge as done or not done, no expertise required. Legible capability is far harder to over- or under-state.

  • CoffeeBench: find a cup, load a pod, operate the machine, deliver the cup to a target zone (the Wozniak coffee test, made reproducible); randomize machine layout, cup/pod positions, target.
  • GroceryBench: unpack a bag of mixed groceries and shelve each item correctly; randomize item set, packing order, shelf layout.
  • TableBench: lay a place setting to a target arrangement; randomize starting positions, orientation, setting style.
  • DishBench: load mixed dishware into a rack without collisions or breakage; randomize dish types, counts, poses.
  • TidyBench: return scattered household objects to designated storage; randomize object set, clutter, target bins.
  • PourBench: pour a target volume into a specified vessel without overshoot or spill; randomize vessel, fill target, placement.

03 / why this exists

There's no standard way to measure a robot's real capabilities.

No shared benchmarks. No public baseline. Nobody outside the labs can see it.

This registry is the front end of the public record we're building. It starts empty by design; filling it, benchmark by benchmark, is what this program is for.

04 / the rule that defines this program

Benchmark = distribution

A task instance is a distribution, not a scene.

A task instance is a recipe: named variables with stated distributions. Realize it under a seed to get one fair sample. Re-seed for a new sample of the same task. That is how you measure generalization, not memorization.

Recipe

KitchenBench.v1
panel     460 × 300 mm  (fixed)
n_ports   ~ Categorical{3, 4, 5}
pos       ~ Grid(3×2) ⊕ U(±21mm)
            gap ≥ 96mm
θ         ~ Uniform(−30°, +30°)
type      ~ Categorical{USB-C,
            Barrel, XLR, RJ45, BNC}
clutter   ~ Bernoulli(0.4)
lighting  ~ Uniform(2700, 6500 K)
──────────────────────────
sample = realize(KitchenBench.v1,
                   seed)

06 / how the program runs

      
Three stages, from proposal to replicated benchmark.

      

        

          Stage 1 · Aug 12 to Sep 13
          
Proposal

          
The benchmark you want to build, the area it addresses, your background, and one fully specified task instance in the published format. Scored on team capability, idea quality, and relevance.

        

        

          Stage 2 · Sep 21 to Oct 9
          
Simulated benchmark

          
Up to 30 teams. A working simulated benchmark in Inspect Robots on the KitchenBench pattern, installing clean and passing CI. No physics simulation required yet: this stage tests whether you can specify a benchmark rigorously.

        

        

          Stage 3 · Oct 21 to Dec 18
          
Physics sim and hardware

          
Up to 12 teams, roughly 40% of Stage 2. Both halves of the deliverable: a physics simulation of the task family and the hardware benchmark itself, each running end to end with a baseline evaluated.

08 / questions

      
Frequently asked.

      

        
Who can apply?
Open worldwide, to individuals and teams, students included. You do not need to own a robot: every team that reaches Stage 3 is sent a fully assembled SO-101 at no cost and keeps it. Everyone enters the same way, with a Stage 1 proposal.

        
Is this a job, a contract, or a grant?
None of those. These are competition awards, not payment for services: no employment or contracting relationship, no supervision, no hours reporting, and no work assigned by us. You choose your own task, method, and schedule, and are judged against criteria published on day one. Awards are flat amounts tied to deliverables, not to time spent.

        
I hold a student visa. Can I enter?
We believe this structure is compatible with the visa status of international students, who are a large share of the robotics research community. Rules vary by institution and by circumstance, so confirm with your international student office before entering. Better to check and enter with confidence than find a problem later.

        
How does the funnel work, and what are the odds?
One funnel, three stages, entered the same way by everyone: a proposal at Stage 1, a simulated benchmark at Stage 2 (up to 30 teams), then the physics simulation and the hardware benchmark at Stage 3 (up to 12 teams). Roughly 40% of Stage 2 teams advance to Stage 3. We state that rate up front so teams can plan. Accepted Stage 2 benchmarks are published whether or not the team advances.

        
What does a Stage 1 entry need to contain?
A proposal: the benchmark you want to build, the target area it addresses (jobs impact, AI timelines, or societal relatability), and your background. Plus one fully specified task instance in the published format: a stochastic setup with named random variables and their distributions, not one fixed scene; a goal written as a natural-language success criterion; a reset procedure; and a success scorer. Due 13 September 2026. Scored on team capability, idea quality, and relevance. Spam, AI slop, and harmful eval proposals are rejected without review.

        
What is due at Stage 2 and Stage 3?
        
    
          
  • Stage 2 A working simulated benchmark in Inspect Robots, on the KitchenBench pattern: instances as distributions, realizations by seed, a scorer, a mock embodiment. The repository must install clean, run end to end, pass CI, and carry a self-score against the published rubric. No physics simulation needed.
    • 
          
  • Stage 3 Both halves: a physics simulation of the task family and the hardware benchmark, each running end to end in Inspect Robots, with a baseline model evaluated and a full reproducibility package covering environment specs with tolerances, the eval protocol, the reset procedure, and rollout counts. Due 18 December 2026, accepted on a rolling basis from 1 December.
    • 
        

        
What does each stage pay?
Stage 1 pays nothing. Stage 2 pays $1,000, plus $500 for each team member beyond the first, to a maximum of $3,000 per team, with $500 in compute credits. Stage 3 pays $11,000 in December on verified delivery, and $8,000 to $20,000 in February on passing replication, set by a rubric published at launch, plus $1,000 in compute credits, up to $1,000 for a bill of materials against receipts, and the SO-101 arms.

        
How are awards paid out?
To the team, by international transfer, split as the team directs. Teams affiliated with a university may instead request payment as a research gift to their institution. Ask for that route at Stage 2 selection, since institutional contracting takes several weeks.

        
How are awards taxed?
Awards may be reported to tax authorities. Awards to recipients outside the United States may be subject to withholding at the applicable rate, recoverable only by filing a US return the following year. Recipients inside the United States receive awards without withholding and may owe estimated tax. We collect tax forms at Stage 3 selection, before any award is disbursed. If you are unsure of the effect, take advice before entering.

        
Which robot do I build on?
The SO-101 is the default reference embodiment. Every Stage 3 team is sent a fully assembled set of two follower arms, ordered on selection day and delivered within a week, free and with no return obligation. They sit outside the $1,000 bill-of-materials cap, which covers task materials and 3D-printable fixtures, files included. Teams may instead propose an alternative embodiment they already own, approved at Stage 2:

        
    
          
  • Arms YAM, WidowX AI (Trossen), Franka Research 3
    • 
          
  • Humanoids AgiBot X1, Unitree G1
    • 
        

        
Do I need to teleoperate the arm or collect demonstrations?
No. Teams drive the arms through inspect-robots-so101, which wraps LeRobot checkpoints (ACT, SmolVLA, π0, diffusion) as an Inspect Robots policy and the SO-ARM follower as an embodiment. A public checkpoint such as lerobot/smolvla_base is enough to produce a baseline. No teleoperation or demo collection is required, and no leader arms are supplied.

        
How does replication work?
We do not rerun every rollout. We spot-check, by a procedure published on day one: 2 rollouts on each of 5 instances, sampled at random and not announced in advance. The test is whether an independent operator can arrange each scene from your written setup alone, without contacting you, and get a result consistent with your claim. It passes if observed successes fall inside the 90% binomial interval implied by your claimed per-instance success rate, and if every sampled instance is realizable from the written spec. Where a rerun falls outside tolerance, we iterate with you before assigning a final tier. This tests reproducibility. It is not a re-estimate of your success rate.

        
Who checks that the task instances are any good?
We commission that separately, rather than assuming teams have done it. Three experts rate each instance on representativeness and quality. An instance is accepted only if both scores are at least 4.

        
Who owns the benchmark I build?
You do. Copyright stays with the authors. Entering grants us a licence to publish the benchmark in WorldEvals under the MIT licence, with your team credited, and to reproduce and report results from it. You keep publication rights in full: write up your own benchmark, submit it anywhere, present it however you like, without clearing anything with us.

        
What is the joint paper?
After replication closes in February we write up the full collected suite: the hardware benchmarks, their paired physics simulations, the measured sim-to-real gap across them, and baseline results for current VLA policies. Submission is targeted for Q1 2027, with a preprint at the same time. Every Stage 3 team that passes replication is offered co-authorship. Teams may decline and be acknowledged instead. Author order and the contribution statement are settled with participating teams beforehand.

        
What are Inspect Robots, KitchenBench, and WorldEvals?
Inspect Robots is RoboCurve's open-source framework for evaluating AI and robots in the physical world. KitchenBench is the worked reference build, published on day one. WorldEvals is the public repository where finished benchmarks are released. The program is a joint effort of ARIA, Constellation, and RoboCurve.
