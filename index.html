01 / Why this exists

There's no standard way to measure a robot's real capabilities.

Every frontier lab scores its own systems on its own private tasks, then ships the demo that looks best.

There's no shared, reproducible way to compare them — so forecasters, policymakers, and the public are left guessing at how capable robots really are and how quickly that's changing. We can't govern or prepare for what we can't measure, and right now almost no one has a reliable read on robotics progress.

No shared benchmark. No public baseline. No way to check the demo.

02 / What we're building together

A public repository of reproducible robotics evaluations.

We're funding teams to build benchmarks designed for frontier labs to adopt — rigorous enough to trust, open enough for anyone to run, and reproducible enough to compare across models and over time. The finished repository is released publicly as a trusted means to gauge how robotics is advancing.

Built on Inspect Robots, the shared evaluation harness. Program run by RoboCurve.

04 / The rule that defines this program

A task instance is a distribution, not a fixed scene.

Anyone can build a robot that nails one rehearsed setup. That measures memorization, not capability. So every benchmark here is a recipe: named random variables with stated distributions, realized under a seed. Change the seed, get a new-but-fair instance. That's how we measure generalization instead of a single lucky run.

  • GoalWhat success looks like, in plain terms.
  • Setup varsEach named, each with a stated distribution.
  • TargetThe outcome to be achieved.
  • Success criteriaHow a rollout is scored.
Every Stage 1 application must include one fully specified task instance in exactly this format.
kitchenbench / pour_pasta / cup-to-bowl Draft 
TaskInstance(
  instance_id = "pour_pasta/cup-to-bowl",
  goal        = "pour the dry pasta into the {vessel}",
  setup = {
    "vessel":         Categorical(("bowl","cup","pot")),
    "fill_g":         Uniform(80, 200),
    "pour_height_cm": Uniform(8, 15),
    "vessel_x_cm":    Normal(0.0, 3.0),
    "vessel_y_cm":    Normal(0.0, 3.0),
  },
  target_kind = "pour_into",
)

# realize(seed=0)  vessel=bowl fill_g=156
#               pour_height_cm=9.9 x=+0.3 y=-1.6
# realize(seed=2)  vessel=cup  fill_g=111
#               pour_height_cm=10.1 x=-7.3 y=+5.4

09 / Questions

Frequently asked.

Who can apply?

Teams building robotics evaluations. You pick one of two tracks — Simulation or Real-world hardware — and one of three target areas: impact on jobs, AI timelines, or societal awareness. Strong newcomers and established researchers are both welcome; what matters is a rigorous, reproducible benchmark.

Do I apply as a team or an individual?

Awards are made per team, with up to 15 teams in the Simulation track and up to 10 in the Hardware track. Bring your own team, or come to the in-person launch hackathon in early September 2026 to form one.

What exactly does a Stage 1 submission need to contain?

An evaluation proposal that includes one fully specified task instance in our required format: a goal, setup variables each with a stated distribution, a target, and success criteria. The instance must be a distribution — a seeded recipe — not a single fixed scene. Hardware proposals must also run on an accepted reference robot, stay within the roughly $1,000 bill-of-materials cap, and specify environment tolerances, a reset procedure, and a rollout count.

How do payments and milestones work?

Stage 1 pays $500 for an accepted proposal that advances (genuine-but-not-selected proposals receive $250, capped at 50; spam or harmful submissions receive $0). Stage 2 pays by milestone. Simulation: up to $2,000 total on delivery, plus $1,000 in GPU credits on acceptance. Hardware: $2,000 on spec approval, $8,000 on a working benchmark, and $5,000–$20,000 on passing replication and quality tier, plus $2,000 in GPU credits upfront and up to $1,000 in materials reimbursed against receipts. All figures are per-team maximums.

How is the final hardware quality-tier amount decided?

By a rubric published on the first day of the program, so you know the scoring standard before you build. The replication-plus-quality-tier milestone pays between $5,000 and $20,000 based on that rubric.

Which robots can I use for a hardware benchmark?

An accepted reference robot. Arms: YAM, SO-101, WidowX AI (Trossen), Franka Research 3. Humanoids: AgiBot X1, Unitree G1. The designated reference embodiment is published on day one.

How are benchmarks licensed and released?

Everything is released open. Code is licensed under MIT; data and documentation under CC-BY. The full public benchmark repository is released after independent replication, in mid-January 2027.

What does "an instance is a distribution" actually mean?

Instead of one rehearsed scene, you define named random variables with stated distributions, realized under a seed. Each seed produces a new but fair instance of the same task. This measures whether a system generalizes, rather than whether it memorized one setup.

What is Inspect Robots, and who runs the program?

Inspect Robots is the shared evaluation harness that Simulation-track benchmarks are built into. The program is run by RoboCurve.
