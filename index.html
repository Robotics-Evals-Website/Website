02 / requirements

What Stage 3 must ship.

  • Runs on an accepted reference robot: the SO-101 by default, single-arm or bimanual
  • Ships a physics simulation of the same task family alongside the hardware benchmark
  • Stays within the $1,000 materials budget (bill of materials, excluding the robot), reimbursed against receipts
  • Specifies precise environment tolerances and success criteria
  • Includes a repeatable reset procedure
  • States a rollout count — how many scored attempts per instance — so every score carries an error bar

You don’t need to own a robot to apply: every team that reaches Stage 3 is sent two fully assembled SO-101 follower arms, ordered on selection day, free and yours to keep even if you withdraw.

03 / three areas we fund

What to build.

Build a benchmark in one of these three areas. The examples are inspiration, not a menu; your own idea is just as welcome.

A

Impact on jobs

Economically meaningful, contact-rich manipulation: the work robots would be paid to do. Measuring it shows when automation crosses from demo to deployment.

  • CableBench: seat multiple cables and barrel/RJ connectors into a port panel; randomize port position, orientation, panel tilt, connector type, cable slack.
  • TorqueBench: drive fasteners to a specified torque without cross-threading; randomize size, head type, hole location, approach angle.
  • FoldBench: fold a laid-out garment to a target footprint; randomize fabric, size, and initial crumple state.
Three more ideas for impact on jobs
  • ClutterPick: retrieve a named item from a bin under heavy occlusion; randomize clutter density, item pose, distractors.
  • HarnessRoute: route a flexible wire harness through a sequence of clips and channels; randomize clip positions, order, stiffness.
  • PlateBench: portion and arrange food-like items to a target layout; randomize count, size, start positions.
B

AI timelines

The physical work behind AI progress that still needs human hands: building robots, servicing compute, running experiments. Every step a machine takes unassisted removes a bottleneck from the loop.

  • SelfBuildBench: assemble a working copy of a reference arm from printed parts and stock hardware; randomize part presentation, fastener set, assembly order.
  • MechanicBench: restore another robot to working order by swapping a battery, replacing a worn part, or tightening loose fixtures; randomize the fault set and component positions.
  • RackBench: mount a compute unit into rails and connect power and data; randomize rail height, unit depth, cable dressing.
Three more ideas for AI timelines
  • HotSwapBench: remove and re-seat a hot-swappable module in a live rack; randomize bay, latch mechanism, insertion force.
  • LabBench: execute a wet-lab protocol step (pipette, weigh, transfer) to a stated tolerance; randomize vessel layout, volumes, tool positions.
  • ReloadBench: replace a depleted consumable (spool, filter, cartridge) and confirm restored operation; randomize type, seating depth.
C

Societal awareness

Tasks anyone can watch and judge as done or not done, no expertise required. Legible capability is far harder to over- or understate.

  • CoffeeBench: find a cup, load a pod, operate the machine, deliver the cup to a target zone (the Wozniak coffee test, made reproducible); randomize machine layout, cup/pod positions, target.
  • GroceryBench: unpack a bag of mixed groceries and shelve each item correctly; randomize item set, packing order, shelf layout.
  • TableBench: lay a place setting to a target arrangement; randomize starting positions, orientation, setting style.
Three more ideas for societal awareness
  • DishBench: load mixed dishware into a rack without collisions or breakage; randomize dish types, counts, poses.
  • TidyBench: return scattered household objects to designated storage; randomize object set, clutter, target bins.
  • PourBench: pour a target volume into a specified vessel without overshoot or spill; randomize vessel, fill target, placement.

04 / why this exists

05 / task instances

Task instance = distribution

A task instance is a distribution, not a scene.

A task instance is a recipe: named variables with stated distributions. Realize it under a seed to get one fair sample. Re-seed for a new sample of the same task. That is how you measure generalization, not memorization.

Recipe

CableBench.v1
panel     460 × 300 mm  (fixed)
n_ports   ~ Categorical{3, 4, 5}
pos       ~ Grid(3×2) ⊕ U(±21mm)
            gap ≥ 96mm
θ         ~ Uniform(−30°, +30°)
type      ~ Categorical{USB-C,
            Barrel, XLR, RJ45, BNC}
clutter   ~ Bernoulli(0.4)
lighting  ~ Uniform(2700, 6500 K)
──────────────────────────
sample = realize(CableBench.v1,
                   seed)

07 / questions

      
Questions.

      

        
Who can apply?
Individuals and teams, students included. You do not need to own a robot: every team that reaches Stage 3 is sent two fully assembled SO-101 arms at no cost and keeps them. Everyone enters the same way, with a Stage 1 proposal, and you may submit more than one.

        
What do I submit at each stage?
        
    
          
  • Stage 1 A proposal: the benchmark you want to build, the area it addresses, your background, and one fully specified task instance in the published format, which is a stochastic setup with named variables and their distributions, a goal, a reset procedure, and a success scorer. Due 13 September 2026, 23:59 Pacific. Scored on team capability, idea quality, and relevance.
    • 
          
  • Stage 2 A working simulated benchmark in Inspect Robots, on the KitchenBench pattern. It must install clean, run end to end, pass CI, and carry a self-score against the published rubric. No physics simulation needed at this stage.
    • 
          
  • Stage 3 A physics simulation of the task family and the hardware benchmark, each running end to end with a baseline evaluated, plus a reproducibility package covering environment specs with tolerances, the evaluation protocol, the reset procedure, and rollout counts. Due 18 December 2026, 23:59 Pacific, rolling from 1 December. We then spot-check a random sample of instances for reproducibility before assigning the tier.
    • 
        

        
Is this a job, a contract, or a grant?
None of those. These are competition awards, not payment for services: no employment or contracting relationship, no supervision, no hours reporting, and no work assigned by us. You choose your own task, method, and schedule, and are judged against criteria published on day one. Awards are flat amounts tied to deliverables, not to time spent. If you hold a student visa, confirm with your international student office before entering.

        
How are awards paid and taxed?
To the team, by international transfer, split as the team directs. University-affiliated teams may instead request payment as a research gift to their institution; ask at Stage 2 selection, since institutional contracting takes weeks. Awards may be reported to tax authorities, and awards paid to teams outside the United States may be subject to US withholding, which may be recoverable by filing a US return the following year. We collect tax forms at Stage 3 selection.

        
Which robot do I build on?
The SO-101 is the default reference embodiment. Every Stage 3 team is sent two fully assembled follower arms, ordered on selection day, free and with no return obligation, including if you withdraw. They sit outside the $1,000 materials budget, which covers task materials and 3D-printable fixtures. You drive them through inspect-robots-so101, which wraps policy checkpoints from LeRobot, Hugging Face’s robot-learning library, as an Inspect Robots policy; a public checkpoint is enough for a baseline, so no teleoperation, demo collection, or leader arms are needed. You may instead propose an alternative embodiment you already own, approved at Stage 2, and still receive the arms:

        
    
          
  • Arms YAM, WidowX AI, Franka Research 3
    • 
          
  • Humanoids AgiBot X1, Unitree G1
    • 
        

        
What do I keep, and what gets published?
You keep copyright and publication rights in full: write up your own benchmark, submit it anywhere, without clearing anything with us. Entering grants us a licence to publish it in WorldEvals under MIT, with your team credited. After replication closes in February we write up the collected suite, the paired simulations, the measured sim-to-real gap, and baseline results for current vision-language-action (VLA) policies, targeting submission in Q1 2027. Every Stage 3 team that passes replication is offered co-authorship, and may decline and be acknowledged instead.

        
What are Inspect Robots, KitchenBench, and WorldEvals?
Inspect Robots is RoboCurve’s open-source framework for evaluating AI and robots in the physical world. KitchenBench is the worked example of what a Stage 2 submission should look like. WorldEvals is the public repository where finished benchmarks will be released.
