§ Why Theory of change

We can't govern what we can't measure.

Robotics companies are standing up massive data-collection operations and unlocking new training data — including web-scale video. Physical capability is rising fast, and robots are expected to soon help build and run the data centers that train the next models, folding physical progress back into AI progress.

Yet everyone outside the labs — forecasters, policymakers, the public — has almost no situational awareness of the questions that matter most: how soon can robots do real jobs, and how quickly is the frontier moving? Shared, reproducible benchmarks are the instrument that answers them. This program funds a repository of them that frontier labs will actually use.

Whether it would ever be strategic for an advanced AI to act against humanity depends heavily on how much that AI still relies on humans for physical labor. Measuring where robotics actually stands is a prerequisite for reasoning about that dependence at all. — after Ajeya Cotra, on self-sufficient AI

§ Areas Three questions a benchmark can help answer

What we want built, and why.

We fund task families that speak to one of three questions. Each area comes with a flagship example — a concrete task we would fund on day one — to signal the bar and the shape of a good submission.

AImpact on jobs

Which real-world tasks can robots already do?

To forecast the physical automation of labor, we need benchmarks that stand in for economically meaningful work — contact-rich, precise, and varied enough that success means capability, not memorization.

CableBench Insert several cables and barrel connectors into a port panel, with port position and orientation randomized each instance.
BAI timelines

Can robots start closing the loop on their own progress?

Capabilities that let robots build, maintain, and operate infrastructure bear directly on how fast AI can accelerate — and on how much frontier systems still depend on human hands.

MechanicBench Robots performing maintenance on other robots: swap a battery, replace a part, tighten loose fixtures, re-seat a component.
CSocietal relatability

Can the public see the progress for themselves?

Progress only informs policy if people can grasp it. We want benchmarks anyone recognizes instantly — legible milestones that make an abstract capability curve concrete.

CoffeeBench Find a cup → insert a pod → operate the machine → deliver the cup to a target zone. The Steve Wozniak coffee test, made reproducible.

§ Format Required shape for every Stage 1 submission

A task instance is a distribution, not a scene.

A fixed scene measures memorization. We fund benchmarks whose instances are recipes: named random variables with stated distributions, realized under a seed. Running a policy across realizations estimates an instance success probability, P̂. Every Stage 1 application must include one instance in exactly this form — that single specified instance is the application.

kitchenbench / pour_pasta / measuring-cup-to-bowl Draft — not human-validated 
TaskInstance(
    instance_id = "pour_pasta/measuring-cup-to-bowl",
    goal        = "pour the dry pasta into the {vessel}",
    setup = {
        "vessel":         Categorical(("bowl", "cup", "pot")),
        "fill_g":         Uniform(80, 200),
        "pour_height_cm": Uniform(8, 15),
        "vessel_x_cm":    Normal(0.0, 3.0),
        "vessel_y_cm":    Normal(0.0, 3.0),
    },
    target_kind = "pour_into",
)
realize(seed=0) vessel        = bowl
fill_g        = 156
pour_height_cm = 9.9
vessel_x_cm   = +0.3
vessel_y_cm   = −1.6
realize(seed=2) vessel        = cup
fill_g        = 111
pour_height_cm = 10.1
vessel_x_cm   = −7.3
vessel_y_cm   = +5.4

5 instances per task  ·  5 realizations per instance  ·  accepted only at ≥ 4/5 from 3 independent raters on representativeness and quality

§ Risks The objections, taken seriously

Two things could go wrong. Here's why we think the trade is worth it.

Won't public benchmarks just hand robotics labs targets to hill-climb on?

  • The marginal information is asymmetric.

    Frontier labs already run extensive internal evals. What public benchmarks add for them is small; what they add for policymakers, forecasters, and the public — who have almost none — is large. The main effect is better oversight, not faster labs.

  • Overfitting is detectable.

    Gains on a fixed suite often reflect tuning to the suite, not real capability. But that's visible: held-out tasks let us separate labs that genuinely improved from labs that optimized against the public set.

  • The task space is enormous.

    A few benchmarks cover a vanishing slice of physically useful work. Stochastic instances — randomized positions, dimensions, configurations — make each a measure of generalization, not a memorization target.

What if the submissions are low-quality, or not aligned to real risk cases?

This is the likely failure mode of an open call, and we address it at every stage.

Website

Clear writing on the three target areas and the task families we want, plus fully specified sample benchmarks. Every application must include one instance in that exact format — a high bar to even apply.

Selection

Proposals scored on team capability, idea quality, and relevance. Only proposals clearing the bar advance. We'd rather run a smaller Stage 2 than fill slots with weak work.

Payout

Milestone-gated. Fail to deliver a working benchmark and you don't reach M2; fail replication and you don't reach M3. The rubric was published on day one.

And if we end up with only a handful of excellent benchmarks? That's still strongly positive on the margin in a field that has almost none — and it tells us that distributed benchmark creation is hard, which directly informs how RoboCurve allocates its own engineering effort.
