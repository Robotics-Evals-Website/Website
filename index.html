Instruction · Goal · Rubric · Setup

Every task has four parts

A benchmark is a list of tasks. One task is written out in full below.

1 Instruction
fold-towel-quarters

What the robot is asked to do, in natural language.

Fold the towel into quarters.

2 Goal
end state

The finished scene, in natural language, described precisely enough that two people scoring the same attempt agree.

The towel is folded flat into quarters, with edges aligned (no side protruding more than 5% of the folded edge length), and the surface is smooth, with no lumps or wrinkles.

3 Scoring rubric
0 → 1

How much credit one attempt earns, between 0 and 1.

Read from the top. The first line that matches sets the score, so an attempt that only touched the towel scores 0.10.

This rubric is discrete. A continuous one is also allowed, where a measured quantity maps onto the same 0–1 range.

  1. 1.00The goal is met.
  2. 0.75Folded into quarters, but with wrinkles, lumps, or protrusion.
  3. 0.50Folded at least once.
  4. 0.20Grasped and lifted at least once.
  5. 0.10Touched.

0Otherwise.

4 Setup
realize(seed=—)

Where everything starts, in natural language. Anything that varies is named and given a distribution.

A towel is placed at (X, Y) on the tabletop relative to the actor. X and Y are independently distributed as N(0, σ = 10 cm).

benchmark  FoldBench.v1
  task       fold-towel-quarters
  table      1200 × 900 mm    (fixed)
  towel       420 × 260 mm    (fixed)
  origin     actor reference point
  X        ~ N(0, 10 cm)
  Y        ~ N(0, 10 cm)      independent
  θ        ~ U(−25°, +25°)
             redraw if the towel leaves the table
  ────────────────────────────────────
  instance = realize(task, seed)
seed = — 

                      

                    

                  


                  

                    

                      
                    

                    
Top‑down. The rings are the 1σ and 2σ contours of the placement distribution; the curves on the near and left edges are the marginal densities of X and Y; the small dots are 96 other draws from it. The dashed cross is the pair of fold axes the goal implies.

                  


                  
Every seed draws a different setup from the same task. Features 1 to 3 do not change. Scoring a policy across many seeds tests whether it can do the task, not whether it has memorized one scene.

One check-in, four weeks in

            
By 30 October your repository should install and run one task end to end. Teams that pass are paid $5,000. Teams that have not started, or that we cannot reach, are removed from the programme and not paid. You keep the arms either way.

          


          

            
We run it again

            
In January and February we re-run part of your benchmark on our own arms.
              We pick one task at random, draw five instances of it, and run three rollouts on each.

            
It passes if we can set up every scene from your written spec alone, and our success rate
              falls inside the 90% interval your own numbers imply. If it passes, we pay the $10,000 and you go on the joint paper as an author. We do not tell you which task we picked, or when we will run it.

What to build.

        
Build a benchmark in one of these three areas. The examples below are there to give you ideas. You can propose something else.

      


      


      
      

        

          
          
Impact on jobs

        

        
Physical work that people are paid to do. If robots get good at it, jobs change, and we want to measure that so people can prepare.


        
    
          
  • CableBench: seat multiple cables and barrel/RJ connectors into a port panel. Port position, orientation, panel tilt, connector type and cable slack all vary.
    • 
          
  • TorqueBench. Drive fasteners to a specified torque without cross-threading. Vary the size, head type, hole location and approach angle.
    • 
          
  • FoldBench asks the robot to fold a laid-out garment to a target footprint. Fabric, size and initial crumple state change between instances.
    • 
        

        
Three more ideas for impact on jobs
          
    
            
  • ClutterPick: retrieve a named item from a bin under heavy occlusion, with clutter density, item pose and distractors randomized.
    • 
            
  • HarnessRoute. Route a flexible wire harness through a sequence of clips and channels. Randomize the clip positions, their order, and the stiffness of the harness.
    • 
            
  • PlateBench. Portion and arrange food-like items to a target layout. Count, size and start positions vary.
    • 
          

        


        
      


      
      

        

          
          
AI timelines

        

        
Building AI takes physical work: data centres, hardware, experiments in labs. Robots that could do it would make AI itself faster to build.


        
    
          
  • RackBench: mount a compute unit into rails and connect power and data. Rail height, unit depth and cable dressing vary between instances.
    • 
          
  • HotSwapBench. Remove and re-seat a hot-swappable module in a live rack, randomizing the bay, the latch mechanism and the insertion force.
    • 
          
  • LabBench: execute a wet-lab protocol step (pipette, weigh, transfer) to a stated tolerance. Vessel layout, volumes and tool positions all change.
    • 
        

        
Three more ideas for AI timelines
          
    
            
  • PanelBench. Terminate conductors into a breaker panel to a wiring diagram. Randomize the circuit count, the conductor gauge and the panel orientation.
    • 
            
  • CoolingBench: connect a liquid-cooling loop to a server sled and confirm it holds pressure. Vary the fitting type, the hose routing and the position of the sled.
    • 
            
  • MechanicBench: restore another robot to working order by swapping a battery, replacing a worn part, or tightening loose fixtures. The fault set and the component positions are randomized.
    • 
          

        


        
      


      
      

        

          
          
Societal awareness

        

        
Ordinary tasks from everyday life. Measuring robots on them is how the public gets a real sense of how fast physical AI is moving.


        
    
          
  • CoffeeBench: find a cup, load a pod, operate the machine, deliver the cup to a target zone. This is the Wozniak coffee test, made reproducible. Machine layout, cup and pod positions, and the target all vary.
    • 
          
  • GroceryBench. Unpack a bag of mixed groceries and shelve each item correctly. Randomize the item set, the packing order and the shelf layout.
    • 
          
  • TableBench is a place setting laid to a target arrangement. Vary the starting positions, the orientation, and the style of the setting.
    • 
        

        
Three more ideas for societal awareness
          
    
            
  • DishBench: load mixed dishware into a rack without collisions or breakage. Dish types, counts and poses vary.
    • 
            
  • TidyBench. Return scattered household objects to designated storage. Randomize the object set, how cluttered the room is, and which bins are the targets.
    • 
            
  • PourBench: pour a target volume into a specified vessel without overshoot or spill, randomizing the vessel, the fill target and the placement.
    •

Questions.

      

        
Who can apply?
Individuals and teams, students included. You do not need to own a robot. Every team that reaches Stage 2 is sent two fully assembled SO-101 arms at no cost and keeps them. Everyone enters the same way, with a Stage 1 proposal, and you may submit more than one.

        
What do I submit at each stage?
        
    
          
  • Stage 1 A proposal: the benchmark you want to build, the area it addresses, your background, and the list of tasks it contains. Every task carries all four parts. Due 21 September 2026, 23:59 Pacific. Scored on team capability, idea quality, and relevance.
    • 
          
  • Stage 2 The hardware benchmark running end to end with a baseline evaluated, plus everything listed in the Stage 2 brief above: results for each checkpoint, the records and video for every rollout, and the reproducibility package. Due 18 December 2026, 23:59 Pacific, rolling from 1 December 2026. We then re-run part of the benchmark ourselves, and the replication bonus is released if it passes.
    • 
        

        
Is this a job, a contract, or a grant?
None of those. These are competition awards rather than payment for services. There is no employment or contracting relationship, no supervision, no hours reporting, and no work assigned by us. You choose your own task, method, and schedule, and are judged against criteria published on day one. Awards are flat amounts tied to deliverables, not to time spent. If you hold a student visa, confirm with your international student office before entering.

        
When am I paid?
Three times.

        
    
          
  • $5,000 at the week-four check-in, 30 October 2026.
    • 
          
  • $15,000 on delivery, once your benchmark meets the Stage 2 requirements.
    • 
          
  • $10,000 in February 2027, if replication passes.
    • 
        

        
We collect payment details and tax forms during onboarding, so the first payment is not waiting on paperwork.

        
How are awards paid and taxed?
        
Everything goes to the team lead by international transfer, and the lead splits it as they see fit.

        
    
          
  • Individuals only We cannot pay a company, an LLC, or a university department. The lead has to be able to receive the award personally.
    • 
          
  • Outside the US Awards may be subject to US withholding.
    • 
          
  • US tax residents No withholding is applied, and you may owe estimated tax.
    • 
          
  • Tax forms Collected at Stage 2 selection, before the first payment.
    • 
          
  • Reporting We report awards where required. Forms are issued each January for the year before, so a 2026 payment is reported in January 2027 and the replication bonus, if it is paid in February 2027, in January 2028.
    • 
        

        
Which robot do I build on?
The SO-101, by default. Every Stage 2 team is sent two assembled follower arms on selection day, free and yours to keep, and they sit outside the $1,000 materials budget. You drive them through inspect-robots-so101, which wraps LeRobot checkpoints as an Inspect Robots policy, so a public checkpoint is enough for a baseline and you need no teleoperation or leader arms. You may propose an embodiment you already own instead, approved at Stage 2, and still receive the arms:

        
    
          
  • Arms YAM, WidowX AI, Franka Research 3
    • 
          
  • Humanoids AgiBot X1, Unitree G1
    • 
        

        
What do I keep, and what gets published?
You keep copyright and publication rights in full. Write up your own benchmark and submit it anywhere, without clearing anything with us. Entering grants us a licence to publish it in WorldEvals, the public repository where finished benchmarks are released — code under MIT, documentation, data and video under CC-BY-4.0. Both require anyone reusing your work to credit your team. After replication closes in February we write up the collected suite and the replication results. Every Stage 2 team that passes replication is offered co-authorship, and may decline and be acknowledged instead.
